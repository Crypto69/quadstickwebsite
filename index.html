Today, a profile is a spreadsheet you can’t see into.

A QuadStick is a game controller for people who play with their mouth, lips and chin: sips, puffs, a lip button and a joystick moved with the head. Each game’s setup lives in a dense Google Sheet, and the Sheet keeps three secrets.

  1. You can’t see which sip does what.

    To find out what a puff on the left hole does, you search every row for mp_left_puff. Then you do it again for the next mode, and the next.

    24left_1,normal,mp_left_puff,
    25left_2,normal,mp_center_sip,
    26left_3,normal,mp_left_center_puff,
    27right_1,normal,mp_right_puff,
    28right_2,normal,mp_center_puff,

  2. PlayStation and Xbox use different words.

    The same trigger is right_2 in one sheet and right_trigger in the other. Move a profile to the other console and you rename every row by hand.

    right_2right_trigger

    xA

    left_1left_bumper

  3. One wrong character, and the file won’t load.

    A comma in a mode name reads as the next cell. A line break reads as a new line. Nothing warns you: you find out when the QuadStick won’t read the profile.

    Profile Name,,Build, edit,

    That comma splits the name into two cells.

“That spreadsheet was driving me crazy.”

The studio was built by a QuadStick player. A new QuadStick arrived, with the help of David Nelson at Innovative AT, who supplies adaptive gaming equipment across Australia. Working out how profiles and modes fitted together was hard at first, and the spreadsheet wasn’t clear enough to learn from.

So it became a small app, to configure things faster and to learn how the whole thing works. It grew from there. If it helps you, say hello: feedback and ideas are welcome on GitHub.

The device is the way in.

Click the part you breathe into.

The editor is a photo of the real QuadStick. The three mouthpiece holes, the side tube, the lip button, the joystick and the jacks on the back can all be clicked. Beside it, the mode as a grid: sip and puff, soft and hard, each hole alone and in pairs.

Narrow it to one part.

Point at a part and it lights up, along with everywhere it is used. Click it and the page shows only that part, with a clear way back out.

Rows, when you want the detail.

The raw rows, with the same row numbers as the Google Sheet. Output, how it behaves, input, and your own note. Rows that nothing triggers fold away.

A map of every mode.

An arrow for each way between modes, labelled with the input that does it. A mode you can’t get out of is flagged before it traps you mid-game.

It tells you what’s wrong. And what isn’t.

Every edit is checked the way the QuadStick would read it. Real problems are kept apart from plain facts about your profile, and a profile with an error can’t be exported until it’s fixed.

Onto the QuadStick, with a backup first.

In the desktop app, plug the QuadStick in and press one button. The app changes the drive only when you ask, and backs it up before it does.

  1. 1

    See what’s on it.

    The profiles on the drive, in the order the QuadStick counts them, with the status lights that pick each one.

  2. 2

    Back it up.

    One press saves every file on the drive, with the date. Restore any backup later.

  3. 3

    Tick the profiles you want.

    Choose which ones go on, then press Put my profiles on the QuadStick.

  4. 4

    Unplug when it says so.

    The app tells you when it’s done and safe to unplug.

Rather copy it yourself?

Export a .csv that is byte-for-byte what the official Google Sheet add-on makes, then tick through a five-step checklist, including how to pick the file with the status lights.

A card to tape beside the screen.

No more memorising sips and puffs. Print a reference card with one page per mode, in type big enough to read from across the room, or a one-page summary of the whole profile.

The same teal for sip and orange for puff as on screen, so the card and the app look like the same thing.

Settings and learning

The QuadStick’s own settings, in plain words.

The device keeps its own settings in prefs.csv: sensitivity, the line between soft and hard, and more. Each one is explained, along with what happens if you leave it alone.

Learn how it works.

Nine short pages, with moving pictures, on how sips, puffs, modes and profiles fit together. Read them before you change anything, or come back when something surprises you.

Built to be used with one pointer.

The app is used by people who move a pointer with their mouth or head. So nothing needs two controls held at once, nothing needs dragging or hovering, every target is large, and everything works from the keyboard.

This site follows the same rules. Movement here is optional: turn it off and every word stays where you can read it.

Free, offline, and yours.

No account. No internet connection needed. Nothing is collected or sent: your profiles and backups stay on your own computer.
